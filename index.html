My Development

A progression from biomedical problems to engineered systems.

I did not start with one narrow engineering specialty. Each project added another layer: first learning to integrate sensors and electronics, then taking ownership of mechanical design, then designing hardware for a demanding research environment, and finally combining biomechanics, computer vision, and code to study movement. More recently, I have also taken on greater responsibility coordinating research work and supporting a team.

Early device work

Learning how systems fail, and how to troubleshoot them

My early medical-device work exposed me to sensors, PCBs, soldering, power, communication faults, and subsystem integration. It taught me that engineering is not just making a design work on paper, but understanding why a physical system does or does not work.

Capstone

Moving from contributor to mechanical lead

On the MACRO hand-assessment device, I became responsible for translating user and system needs into physical geometry, adjustable mechanisms, prototypes, and interfaces with sensors and actuators. That experience made mechanical design and prototyping a central part of how I see myself as an engineer.

Research hardware

Designing for real constraints instead of ideal ones

The MRI-compatible ferret cradle pushed me to work around strict spatial, material, imaging, usability, and animal-safety constraints. Repeated build-test-refine cycles taught me to treat validation as part of design rather than something that happens after design.

Current research

Connecting mechanics with quantitative movement

My gait-analysis work expanded my interests into biomechanics, computer vision, and Python. I now think about physical systems from both sides: how they are built and how their motion can be measured, analyzed, and validated.

Growing responsibility

Technical ownership plus team leadership

As my research responsibilities have grown, I have taken on leadership within the ferret research team, coordinating shared work, helping maintain continuity across projects, and supporting teammates while continuing to develop my own research.

How I Work

The themes connecting my projects.

Start with the real constraint

I like understanding the user, environment, geometry, interfaces, and failure modes before committing to a design.

Build, test, learn, repeat

Prototyping and validation are central to my process. I learn the most when a physical design is tested on the system it was made for.

Connect disciplines

My strongest work sits between mechanical design, electronics, biomechanics, software, and research rather than inside only one of them.

About Me

Engineering that improves how people understand, interact with, or use technology.

I completed my BS in Biomedical Engineering at the University of Arizona and am pursuing an accelerated MS in Biomedical Engineering, expected May 2027. My interests include medical devices, mechanical design, biomechanics, robotics, research instrumentation, manufacturing, sensing, and quantitative motion analysis.

I am especially drawn to roles where I can stay close to the hardware: designing components, building prototypes, testing systems, analyzing results, and working with people from different technical backgrounds.
